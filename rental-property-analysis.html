RENTAL PROPERTY ANALYSIS

How to Analyze a Rental Property Before You Buy: The 6 Numbers I Check Now

By Jerry Myers · owner of a 10-unit rental

When I bought my 10-unit, I projected $5,200 a month in rent. On a good month I collected $4,000. Property taxes went from $2,345 to $6,350. Occupancy swung from 40% to 90% and back down to 50%. Over time I lost close to $30,000 in rents and late fees.

I never missed a mortgage payment. But the deal I analyzed and the deal I owned were two different deals. The spreadsheet wasn't wrong because the math was bad. It was wrong because I fed it the wrong numbers.

So here's how I analyze a rental property now. It's six numbers. I call it MARGIN, and I run every one of them before I make an offer.

M — Market rent ceiling

Don't use the rent the seller says the units could get. Find the most a tenant in that exact area will actually pay for that exact unit, today. Look at what's renting, not what's listed.

My projection assumed rents the market didn't fully support. Between that and collections that came up short, I was about $1,200 a month under what I'd planned.

Ask: What are similar units in this neighborhood actually renting for right now?

A — Assessed taxes & insurance

This is the one that hurt me most. I underwrote the seller's tax bill. After I bought, the property was reassessed, and $2,345 became $6,350. That's about $333 a month straight out of cash flow.

Call the county. Ask how they reassess after a sale. Then model taxes at 2x what the seller pays and see if the deal still works. Get a real insurance quote, not last year's number from the seller.

Ask: What will the tax bill be after I buy it, not before?

R — Real collections

Rent roll and collections are not the same thing. Late payments, partial payments, and tenants who leave owing you money all come out of the gap between them.

Ask for bank deposits, not just the rent roll. Twelve months if you can get it.

Ask: How much rent actually hit the bank each month last year?

G — Gaps in occupancy

Most analysis tools default to 5% vacancy. My building went 40% to 90% to 50%. Turnover, repairs between tenants, and slow seasons all add up.

Run your numbers at a vacancy rate that would actually scare you, then see if you can still pay every bill.

Ask: If three units sit empty for two months, can I still cover the mortgage?

I — Interest & financing

Know your real payment: rate, term, balloon dates, adjustments, and what happens if you have to refinance. A deal that works at one rate can break at another.

Ask: Does this deal still work if my financing costs go up?

N — Necessary reserves

Something will break. Roofs, furnaces, water heaters, plumbing. Reserves are what let you get through a bad stretch without missing a payment. They're the reason I never missed one.

Ask: How many months of expenses can I cover with nothing coming in?

The point of all six

Miss one and the deal doesn't die fast. It dies slow, a few hundred dollars a month at a time. Run all six before you make an offer, and use numbers you've verified yourself, not the seller's.
